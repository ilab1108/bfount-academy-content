Quick answer: Negative keywords tell Google Ads which searches should not show your ads. You add them as broad, phrase or exact negatives at campaign, ad group or account level, or in shared lists. Unlike normal keywords, negatives do not match close variants, so plurals, misspellings and synonyms each need their own entry. Your search terms report is where you find them. Check it every week, add the obvious junk, and be careful not to block searches that actually convert.

For lead generation and pay-per-call campaigns, negatives can separate a profitable account from one that pays for "jobs", "free" and "how to" clicks every day. This guide covers how negatives work, a decision table for match types, a starter list you can adapt, and a weekly routine.

What negative keywords are

A keyword tells Google which searches you want to show up for. A negative keyword does the opposite. If you run a "plumber near me" campaign and add jobs as a negative, someone searching "plumber jobs near me" won't see your ad.

That matters because you pay for clicks, not results. A click from a job seeker, a student or a DIY researcher costs the same as a click from a homeowner with a burst pipe. With fewer wasted clicks, your cost per lead drops, your conversion data gets cleaner, and Smart Bidding learns from people who might actually buy.

Negatives matter even more now that most accounts run on broad match and automated bidding. Broad match finds searches you never thought to target, so expect some of them to be wrong for you. Negatives are how you put limits on it.

Negative match types (and the close-variant trap)

Google Ads has three negative match types. They look like the positive match types, but they behave differently in one important way: negative keywords do not expand to close variants. A negative flower won't block "flowers". A negative cheap won't block "cheapest" or "inexpensive".

Negative match typeHow you enter itBlocks a search when…Example: negative free quote
Negative broad (default)free quoteAll the words appear in the search, in any orderBlocks "quote free roofing", "free roof quote". Does not block "free estimate".
Negative phrase"free quote"The exact words appear in that order, with other words allowed around themBlocks "get a free quote now". Does not block "quote free".
Negative exact[free quote]The search is exactly those words and nothing elseBlocks only "free quote".

Because negatives are literal, add the variants yourself: job and jobs, career and careers, common misspellings, and any synonyms that show up in your reports.

Where to add negatives: ad group, campaign, account, lists

  • Ad group level: best for routing. Keep a search out of one ad group so it goes to another.
  • Campaign level: the most common place. Use it for searches that are wrong for the whole campaign's offer or location.
  • Negative keyword lists (shared library): build a list once, such as "Employment and education" or "Competitors", and apply it to many campaigns. When you update the list, every linked campaign gets the change. This is the cleanest way to run negatives across a large account.
  • Account level: negatives that apply to every eligible campaign in the account, including Performance Max. Use them for terms that are never right for the business, like adult terms, brand-safety terms, or the word "free" if you never give anything away.

Google caps how many negatives each level and list can hold, and it has raised those caps several times. Check the current limits in your account before you plan a very large list. If you're close to a cap, combine your lists.

How to find negative keywords

1. Before launch: brainstorm by intent

Write down the intents that don't match yours: job seekers, students, DIY researchers, people looking for free or cheap versions, people looking for a competitor or a different product, and people outside your service area. Then turn each intent into words. The starter list below does most of this for you.

2. After launch: the search terms report

In Google Ads, go to Insights and reports → Search terms. It shows the actual searches that triggered your ads. Sort by cost and look for:

  • Terms with spend and no conversions over a sensible sample
  • Terms with the wrong intent, even if they've converted once by accident
  • Terms that belong in a different ad group or campaign

Some low-volume searches are hidden from the report for privacy reasons, so you won't see every term you paid for. That's one more reason to block whole categories with single-word negatives, not just the exact phrases you happen to see.

3. From your call and lead data

For pay-per-call, your search terms report only shows half the picture. Pair it with call tracking, so you can see which searches lead to short calls, wrong-number calls, or callers who are after something you don't sell. A search that brings in calls under 30 seconds is a strong negative candidate even when Google counts those calls as conversions.

Negatives in Performance Max and broad match campaigns

For a long time, the only way to block Performance Max from certain searches was to ask Google support or use account-level negatives. That's changed. Google added campaign-level negative keywords to Performance Max in early 2025 and, in spring 2025, raised the limit to 10,000 negatives per campaign, the same as Search campaigns.

A few points to keep in mind:

  • PMax negatives only affect Search and Shopping inventory. They don't stop your ads from showing on YouTube, Display, Gmail or Discover.
  • Brand exclusions are a separate setting. Use them when you want to keep PMax off your own brand or a competitor's brand, rather than typing every brand variant as a negative.
  • Broad match and AI-driven search features need more negatives than a tight phrase- or exact-match account, because they reach further. Review search terms more often in the first weeks after you turn them on.

Don't overdo it either. Automated bidding needs volume to learn. If you block everything that hasn't converted yet, you can starve a campaign and push the price of the clicks you have left higher. The goal is to cut wrong intent, not every search that hasn't converted in a week.

A 20-minute weekly negative keyword routine

  1. Open search terms for the last 7 days (14 or 30 for low-volume accounts). Sort by cost, highest first.
  2. Mark clear wrong-intent terms and add them straight away. Use the single word that causes the problem (jobs), not the full query.
  3. Mark "spend, no conversion" terms, and only act once they've spent at least your target cost per lead with nothing to show. Our Max CPL lab helps you set that number.
  4. Check routing: is each search going to the right ad group? Add ad group negatives where it isn't.
  5. Put repeat offenders on a shared list so new campaigns start out protected.
  6. Check for conflicts: Google Ads flags negatives that block your own active keywords. Fix these before you close the report.
  7. Log what you added with a date. If conversions fall the following week, your log shows what to undo first.

How to estimate what a negative saves

Take the last 30 days of spend on the terms you're about to block. That's your wasted spend if nothing changes. Subtract that waste from total spend and divide by your conversions to see roughly what your cost per lead would have been. Run the result through the PPC Profit lab to see what it does to margin. Clicks you remove also change your auction mix, so treat this as an estimate, not a promise.

Common mistakes

  • Blocking your own keywords. A negative cheap in a campaign that bids on "cheap car insurance quotes" stops it serving. Always check for conflicts.
  • Adding full queries as negative exact. That only blocks one search out of thousands. Find the word that causes the problem.
  • Forgetting variants. Negatives don't match close variants, so job alone leaves jobs open.
  • Negating "free" when your offer is a free quote. Use phrase negatives for the bad combinations ("free download", "for free") instead.
  • Cutting too fast on small data. One click with no conversion isn't a pattern. Set a spend threshold first.
  • Never reviewing the list. Products, offers and locations change. Look over your shared lists every quarter.

Negatives also make your other levers work better. Cleaner traffic means a higher click-through rate, which supports Quality Score, and better conversion data for whichever bidding strategy you run.

FAQ

How many negative keywords should a campaign have?

There's no right number. A tight exact-match campaign might need a few dozen. A broad match or Performance Max campaign in a busy vertical can need hundreds. Let the search terms report decide.

Do negative keywords match plurals and misspellings?

No. Google says negative keywords don't match close variants, so add plurals, misspellings and synonyms separately.

Can negative keywords hurt performance?

Yes, if they block searches that convert or cut volume so far that Smart Bidding can't learn. Check for conflicts and keep a dated log so you can roll changes back.

Should I add competitor names as negatives?

If you don't plan to bid on competitors, yes. Searches for a competitor's login or phone number rarely convert. If you do bid on them, keep those terms in their own campaign and use negatives to keep them out of everything else.

Do negative keywords work on Display and YouTube?

Not in the same way. Keyword negatives are mainly for Search and Shopping. Use content exclusions, placement exclusions and topic exclusions for Display and video.
