Short answer: In pay per call you are paid for conversations, not clicks. That makes keyword research for calls less about search volume and more about intent. Start with action-intent keywords: "get a quote", "near me", "today", "emergency", "enroll", "call". Launch them on phrase and exact match. Add a negative keyword list on day one to block job seekers, DIY researchers and people looking for a government office or customer service line. Then judge every keyword by billable calls, tracked back to the keyword, not by clicks or call volume. This guide covers the three intent layers, modifiers for each vertical, how to use match types, a negative keyword starter list and a scoring sheet you can copy to rank your keyword list before you spend anything.

Why keyword research for calls is different

Most keyword research advice is written for websites that want traffic. Traffic tools rank keywords by monthly searches and difficulty. Pay per call works on a different scale of value. A buyer pays you only when a call meets their rules, usually a minimum duration (the "buffer"), placed within their hours, from a caller in the right area who matches their criteria.

That changes what a "good" keyword is. A keyword that drives lots of cheap clicks but few billable calls is worse than useless. It spends your budget, and if you use smart bidding it trains the algorithm to find more of the wrong people. A small keyword that sends three calls a day, two of them billable, can be worth more than a big one that sends thirty clicks and no qualified callers.

So the question for every keyword is: how likely is someone typing this to want to speak to a business right now, and to qualify for this buyer?

Information-intent searches still have value. They are where people start. Answer them with helpful content and a clear phone number, and some of those readers will call later. Just don't pay per click for them at the start.

Vertical-specific modifiers

Each vertical has its own signals. A few examples:

  • Medicare: "Medicare Advantage plans [city]", "Medicare supplement quotes", "change Medicare plan". Avoid "Medicare phone number" and "Medicare card replacement". Those callers want the government, not an agent, and sending them to a third party can be misleading. Timing matters too: search demand and buyer rules change around the Annual Enrollment Period.
  • Final expense: "burial insurance for seniors", "final expense life insurance quote", "funeral insurance over 70". Age modifiers are strong signals because they match the buyer's eligibility rules.
  • Home services: "[service] near me", "emergency [service]", "[service] repair cost", plus city names. Urgency words ("emergency", "24 hour", "same day") are often the best performers, so make sure the buyer's hours actually cover those calls.
  • Legal: "[injury] lawyer near me", "do I have a case", "settlement for [product]". Many legal terms are among the most expensive keywords on the web. Test carefully, with tight budgets and exact match.

Before you build a list, read the campaign's rules: states, hours, caller age or criteria, and any keyword or brand restrictions. A keyword the buyer doesn't allow, or one that sends callers outside their hours or area, will never pay, however good it looks.

How to use it: 7–8 = launch on exact and phrase match. 5–6 = test in a separate ad group with a lower bid. 4 or below = park it, use it as a content topic, or add it as a negative. A risk score of 0 means don't bid on the keyword at all, whatever the total.

Next to the score, note the estimated cost per click from the keyword planner. Then check it against your break-even CPC: the most you can pay per click given the payout, your click-to-call rate and your billable rate. A keyword that scores 8 but costs twice your break-even CPC needs a better landing page or a higher payout, not a bigger bid.

Match types in practice

Search engines interpret match types loosely now, especially broad match. Exact match can show for close variants with the same meaning, and phrase match can show for searches that contain the meaning of your phrase. A sensible approach for call campaigns:

  • Launch with phrase and exact match on your core action keywords to control spend.
  • Add broad match only in a separate campaign or ad group, paired with smart bidding on billable calls, once you have conversion data. Broad match relies on the conversion signal to learn. Without a clean signal it tends to wander.
  • Promote winning search terms from broad into exact match so they get their own bids.
  • Be careful with automated expansion features such as AI-driven search term matching. Test them the same way as broad match: in their own campaign, with negatives and a firm budget, measured on billable calls.

Grouping matters too. Keep each ad group to one tight theme so the ad and landing page can match the search closely. That also helps your Quality Score and lowers what you pay per click.

A negative keyword starter list

Add these on day one and expand from your search terms report:

  • Jobs and careers: jobs, career, salary, hiring, employment, training, course, license exam
  • Free or DIY: free, diy, how to, template, sample, pdf, download
  • Research: definition, meaning, wiki, statistics, history, essay
  • Wrong audience: login, portal, customer service, cancel, complaint (unless your buyer wants retention calls)
  • Government or brand impersonation risks: official site, .gov, social security office (for most verticals)

Two details catch people out. First, negative keywords don't automatically cover close variants the way positive keywords do. Add plurals and common misspellings yourself: "job" and "jobs", "career" and "careers". Second, put negatives you'll use everywhere in a shared negative keyword list and apply it to every call campaign, so a new campaign never launches without them.

Watch you don't block your own buyers. "Free" may be a negative in most verticals, but "free quote" can be a strong action keyword. Check how a negative interacts with your core list before you add it.

Read the search terms report like a detective

Every few days, sort search terms by cost and ask one question per row: would this person plausibly be a billable caller for my buyer? Add a negative for every "no". Look for patterns. If "medicaid" keeps appearing in a Medicare campaign, add it as a negative across the account.

A simple routine that works:

  1. Filter the last 7 days, sorted by cost, highest first.
  2. Mark each search term as keep, promote (add it as an exact match keyword) or negate.
  3. Negate at the right level: a single odd search at ad group level, a pattern (a word that is always wrong) at campaign or list level.
  4. Write down what you negated and why, so the next person doesn't undo it.

Validate with calls, not clicks

Once calls start, connect each call to the keyword that produced it. Use dynamic number insertion for calls from your landing page, and your ad platform's call reporting for calls straight from the ad. Our guide to call tracking explains how to set this up.

Then look at three numbers per keyword: calls, billable calls and cost per billable call. A keyword that produces many calls but a low billable rate is a candidate for pausing or lower bids. Before you pause it, listen to a few recordings. The problem may be the keyword, or it may be the ad promising something the buyer doesn't offer. Compare the cost per billable call with the payout. If it costs more than the payout, the keyword loses money however good the call volume looks. The PPC profit lab runs that check for you.

Common mistakes

  • Picking keywords by search volume. Big generic terms are usually informational and expensive.
  • Launching broad match with no conversion data. The algorithm has nothing to learn from yet.
  • Bidding on government or brand terms. It confuses callers, risks disapprovals and breaks most buyers' rules.
  • Ignoring buyer hours. An "emergency" keyword at 2 a.m. is wasted if the buyer only takes calls 9–5.
  • Optimising on calls instead of billable calls. Short or unqualified calls look like success in the ad platform but don't pay.

FAQ

What are the best keywords for pay per call?

Action-intent keywords that match the buyer's rules: phrases with "quote", "near me", "call", "today" or "emergency", plus the product and, where relevant, a location or age modifier. Score them with the sheet above and start with the highest totals.

Should I use broad match for call campaigns?

Only once you have reliable billable-call conversion data, and then in a separate campaign or ad group with smart bidding and a strong negative list. Start with phrase and exact match.

How many negative keywords do I need?

There's no fixed number. Start with the starter list above. Expect it to grow fast in the first few weeks as the search terms report shows you what people actually type.

Are informational keywords worth anything in pay per call?

Yes, as content topics. Helpful articles that answer "what is" and "how does" questions can bring in organic visitors who call later. They rarely pay as paid search keywords.

How do I know which keyword drove a call?

Use call tracking with dynamic number insertion on your landing page and the ad platform's call reporting for calls from the ad. Both can attribute calls to keywords.
