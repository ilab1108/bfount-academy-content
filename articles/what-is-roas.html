ROAS (return on ad spend) is the revenue your ads bring in divided by what you spent on them. If you spend $1,000 on ads and they generate $4,000 in sales, your ROAS is 4, often written as 4:1, 4x or 400%. It is the single most-used number for judging whether paid advertising is working — but a "good" ROAS depends entirely on your margins, which is why so many advertisers misread it.

This guide covers the formula, worked examples, how to calculate the minimum ROAS your business needs to break even, and the practical levers that raise it.

1. The ROAS formula

ROAS = Revenue from ads ÷ Ad spend

  • Revenue from ads is the sales value attributed to your campaigns in a period.
  • Ad spend is what you paid the ad platform in the same period.

To express it as a percentage, multiply by 100. A ROAS of 3.5 is the same as 350%.

Two details matter more than the formula itself:

  1. Use the same time window and attribution rules for both numbers. Mixing 30-day revenue with 7-day spend gives a meaningless result.
  2. Decide whether "revenue" means gross sales or revenue after refunds. Pick one and keep it consistent across every report.

2. Worked examples

Example 1 — An online store

A store spends $2,500 on Meta ads in October. The ads are credited with 125 orders at an average order value of $80.

  • Revenue = 125 × $80 = $10,000
  • ROAS = $10,000 ÷ $2,500 = 4.0

Example 2 — A lead-generation business

A home-services company does not sell online; it gets phone calls and form leads. It spends $3,000 on Google Ads and receives 150 leads. Its sales team closes 20% of leads, and an average job is worth $600.

  • Customers = 150 × 20% = 30
  • Revenue = 30 × $600 = $18,000
  • ROAS = $18,000 ÷ $3,000 = 6.0

For lead generation, ROAS only works if you know your close rate and customer value. Without them, track cost per lead and cost per sale instead.

Example 3 — Pay-per-call publisher

A publisher spends $1,200 on Google call ads and delivers 80 billable calls that pay $35 each.

  • Revenue = 80 × $35 = $2,800
  • ROAS = $2,800 ÷ $1,200 = 2.33

3. ROAS vs ROI vs CPA

Metric Formula What it tells you
ROAS Revenue ÷ Ad spend How much revenue each ad dollar returns
ROI (Profit − Total cost) ÷ Total cost Whether the whole activity made money after all costs
CPA Ad spend ÷ Conversions What you pay for each sale, lead or call

ROAS ignores product costs, fees, staff and overheads. A campaign can show a "good" ROAS and still lose money. That is why the next section matters most.

4. How to calculate your break-even ROAS

Your break-even ROAS is the ROAS at which ad-driven sales exactly cover the ad spend and the cost of delivering those sales.

Break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ Profit margin (before ad spend)

Step 1: Work out your margin before advertising. Take the selling price, subtract cost of goods, shipping, payment fees and any per-sale costs, then divide by the price.

Step 2: Divide 1 by that margin.

Margin before ad spend Break-even ROAS
20% 5.0
25% 4.0
33% 3.0
50% 2.0
70% 1.43

Example: You sell a product for $100. Product, shipping and fees cost $60, leaving $40 — a 40% margin. Break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ 0.40 = 2.5. Below 2.5 you lose money on each ad-driven sale; above it you profit.

Set your target ROAS above break-even to leave room for overheads and profit. Many advertisers aim for break-even plus 20–50%, then adjust once they know their real customer lifetime value.

Factor in repeat purchases. If customers buy again, the first sale is not the whole story. A business with strong repeat purchases can accept a first-order ROAS below break-even, as long as lifetime value covers it. Use our LTV:CAC lab to model this.

5. What is a good ROAS?

There is no universal "good" ROAS. A 3:1 ROAS can be excellent for a software company with 80% margins and a loss for a reseller with 20% margins. The right question is: is my ROAS above my break-even, and is it improving?

Factors that move what "good" looks like:

  • Margin — the biggest single factor (see the table above).
  • Funnel stage — retargeting and branded search usually show much higher ROAS than prospecting to cold audiences, because they reach people already close to buying.
  • Attribution window — longer windows credit more revenue to ads.
  • Business model — subscriptions and repeat-purchase brands can accept lower first-purchase ROAS.

Benchmarks you see published online vary widely by source, industry and year. Use them as a rough sense check, never as your target.

6. How to track ROAS correctly

  1. Pass purchase value to the ad platform. On Meta, send the Purchase event with value and currency through both the Pixel and the Conversions API. On Google Ads, set conversion values on your purchase conversion action.
  2. Use UTM parameters on every ad so GA4 can attribute revenue by source, campaign and ad.
  3. Import offline conversions if sales close by phone or in person — upload closed deals back to Google and Meta so they optimise for real revenue, not just leads.
  4. Compare platform-reported ROAS with your own data. Platforms tend to credit themselves generously. Check against revenue in your store, CRM or billing system every month.
  5. Report ROAS by campaign, not just account-wide. An account average hides campaigns that lose money.

7. Seven ways to improve ROAS

  1. Cut waste first. Add negative keywords, exclude poor placements and pause ads below break-even after a fair test.
  2. Improve the landing page. A faster page with a clearer offer and fewer form fields lifts conversion rate — and ROAS — without spending more.
  3. Raise average order value. Bundles, upsells and free-shipping thresholds increase revenue per conversion.
  4. Test new creative regularly. On Meta and TikTok, creative fatigue is a leading cause of falling ROAS.
  5. Bid on value, not volume. Use value-based bidding (target ROAS on Google, highest value on Meta) once you have enough conversion data.
  6. Segment by intent. Separate branded, competitor and generic search; separate prospecting from retargeting. Each deserves its own target.
  7. Follow up faster (see speed to lead). For lead generation, the speed of your first call has a large effect on close rate, and therefore on ROAS.

8. Common ROAS mistakes

  • Judging ROAS without knowing your margin. Always calculate break-even first.
  • Scaling budget too fast. ROAS usually drops as you spend more and reach less-interested audiences. Increase budgets in steps and watch the trend.
  • Trusting one data source. Reconcile ad-platform numbers with your real revenue.
  • Optimising only for ROAS. A very high ROAS on a tiny budget may earn less profit than a lower ROAS on a larger one. Profit is the goal; ROAS is a guide.

9. FAQ

Is ROAS the same as ROI? No. ROAS compares revenue with ad spend only. ROI compares profit with all costs. A positive-looking ROAS can still mean a negative ROI.

How do I calculate ROAS as a percentage? Divide revenue by ad spend and multiply by 100. $5,000 revenue on $1,000 spend is 500%.

What is target ROAS in Google Ads? Target ROAS is a smart bidding strategy where you tell Google the average conversion value you want for each unit of ad spend. Google then sets bids to try to reach that return.

Can ROAS be below 1? Yes. A ROAS below 1 means your ads brought in less revenue than they cost — you lost money on ad spend alone.

How long should I wait before judging ROAS? Wait until a campaign has enough conversions to be reliable and has exited the platform's learning phase. Judging after one or two days usually leads to wrong decisions.
