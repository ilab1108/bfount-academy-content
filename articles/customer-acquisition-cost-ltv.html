Short answer: customer acquisition cost (CAC) is what you spend to win one new customer: CAC = total acquisition cost ÷ new customers. Lifetime value (LTV) is the gross profit a customer brings in over the whole relationship: LTV = average revenue per period × gross margin × average customer lifetime. Compare them as a ratio (LTV : CAC) and as a payback period (how many months of profit it takes to earn back CAC). Then work backwards to the number that matters every day: the maximum you can afford to pay for a lead, which is the CAC you can accept multiplied by your close rate. Every marketing decision eventually comes down to one question — is this customer worth more than it cost to win them? These four numbers answer it.

Customer acquisition cost (CAC)

CAC = total acquisition cost for a period ÷ new customers won in that period

"Total acquisition cost" means everything you spent to win those customers, not just ad spend:

  • Media: ad spend, sponsorships, lead or call purchases.
  • Tools: call tracking, CRM, email software, landing page builders (the share used for acquisition).
  • People: agency or freelancer fees, and the sales and marketing time spent on new customers.
  • Sales commissions and bonuses paid on new deals.

Example: you spend $10,000 in a quarter across ads, tools and commissions, and win 50 new customers. CAC = $10,000 ÷ 50 = $200.

Blended CAC vs paid CAC

Blended CAC divides all acquisition spend by all new customers, including the ones who found you through word of mouth or search. Paid CAC uses only paid spend and only customers who came from paid channels. Blended CAC flatters paid channels if you have strong referrals; paid CAC tells you whether your ads stand on their own. Track both, and compare channels using paid CAC by channel.

Lifetime value (LTV)

LTV = average revenue per period × gross margin × average customer lifetime (in periods)

Example: a customer pays $100 a month, your gross margin is 60%, and customers stay 30 months on average. LTV = $100 × 60% × 30 = $1,800.

Use gross margin, not revenue. If you compare $3,000 of revenue to a $200 CAC, the business looks far healthier than it is, because a large part of that revenue goes to delivering the product or service.

Estimating customer lifetime

  • Subscriptions: average lifetime in months ≈ 1 ÷ monthly churn rate. With 4% of customers cancelling each month, the average lifetime is about 25 months.
  • Repeat purchases (a dentist, a car service, an online store): average orders per customer × average gross profit per order, using your own order history.
  • One-off sales (a roof, a legal case, an insurance policy): LTV is roughly the gross profit of the single sale, plus any renewals or referrals you can actually measure.

If you are new and have little history, use a cautious estimate and replace it with real data after six to twelve months. An LTV built on hope is the most common reason a business overspends on acquisition.

The LTV : CAC ratio

Divide LTV by CAC. In the example, $1,800 ÷ $200 = 9 : 1.

  • Below 1 : 1 — you lose money on every customer. Stop scaling and fix the economics.
  • Around 3 : 1 — a common target many businesses use as a healthy balance between growth and profit.
  • Well above 3 : 1 — you are profitable per customer and may be under-investing in growth; you could afford to bid more for more customers.

The right number depends on your cash flow and margins. A business that waits 18 months to earn back its CAC needs a much higher ratio, or a lot of cash, compared with one that earns it back in two months.

Payback period

Payback period (months) = CAC ÷ monthly gross profit per customer

With a $200 CAC and $60 a month in gross profit per customer ($100 × 60%), payback = $200 ÷ $60 ≈ 3.3 months — just over three months. A shorter payback means the money you spend on acquisition comes back faster and can be reinvested in the next customer. For small businesses without investors, payback is often more important than the ratio: a great LTV doesn't help if you run out of cash before you collect it.

From CAC to maximum cost per lead

Most campaigns are bought and reported per lead or per call, not per customer. To turn your unit economics into a bidding limit, work backwards:

Max CPL = the most you are willing to spend per customer × close rate

Example: you are happy to spend up to $600 to win a customer (one-third of a $1,800 LTV, keeping a 3 : 1 ratio), and your sales team closes 20% of leads. Max CPL = $600 × 20% = $120 per lead.

That gives you a clear rule: a channel delivering leads at $80 has room to scale; one at $150 needs better conversion or a lower price before you add budget. Our guide to cost per lead covers this formula in more depth, and the Max CPL lab lets you test your own numbers.

CAC and LTV for pay per call

If you are a publisher or affiliate sending calls to a buyer, the "customer" is the payable call and the "LTV" is what the buyer pays you for it:

Revenue per call = payout × payable rate

With a $45 payout and 60% of calls meeting the buyer's qualification rules, each call you generate is worth $45 × 60% = $27. Your cost per call — media spend divided by all calls, payable or not — must stay below $27 to make a profit. See break-even CPC for pay per call to take this one step further to the most you can bid per click.

For a buyer, it works the other way round: the payout is your cost per call, and your close rate on those calls and the value of each sale decide whether the campaign is worth running.

How to improve the ratio

Lower CAC

  • Raise conversion rates on landing pages and in sales. The same spend producing more customers lowers CAC directly. Start with landing pages that make the phone ring.
  • Follow up faster. Leads contacted quickly convert better than leads called back the next day — see speed to lead.
  • Cut channels by cost per customer, not cost per lead. Cheap leads that never buy raise CAC.
  • Track properly so you can see which campaigns produce customers — tag links with UTM parameters and track calls.

Raise LTV

  • Keep customers longer with onboarding, check-ins and reminders; lower churn increases lifetime directly.
  • Increase gross margin through pricing or cheaper delivery.
  • Sell more to existing customers — upgrades, add-ons and repeat services — through email and follow-up sequences.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving costs out of CAC — commissions, tools, agency fees, refunds and chargebacks all belong in it.
  • Using platform-reported conversions instead of real sales from your CRM. Ad platforms count leads and sometimes count the same customer twice; your CRM counts customers.
  • Judging channels by cost per lead instead of cost per customer.
  • Using revenue instead of gross profit in LTV, which overstates what you can afford.
  • Mixing time periods — spend from one month divided by customers from another.
  • Ignoring payback — a strong ratio with a 24-month payback can still run a small business out of cash.

FAQ

What is a good LTV to CAC ratio?

Around 3 : 1 is a widely used benchmark, but it is a starting point, not a rule. Businesses with fast payback and strong cash flow can run lower ratios; those with long payback periods usually need higher ones.

What is the difference between CAC and CPA?

CPA (cost per acquisition) usually means the cost per conversion as an ad platform defines it — which might be a lead, a sign-up or a sale. CAC always means the full cost of winning a paying customer, including non-media costs.

Should CAC include salaries?

For a full, blended CAC, yes: include the share of sales and marketing salaries spent on winning new customers. For comparing ad channels against each other, many teams use paid CAC (media and direct campaign costs only) so the comparison is fair.

How do I calculate LTV with no history?

Start with a conservative estimate — for example, one year of gross profit or the profit on a single sale — and update it as real retention and repeat-purchase data comes in.

How often should I recalculate CAC and LTV?

Monthly for CAC by channel, and quarterly for LTV. Recalculate both after any big change in pricing, offer or sales process.
