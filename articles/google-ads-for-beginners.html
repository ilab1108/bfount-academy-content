To launch a first Google Ads campaign that can actually make money, start with a Search campaign in Expert Mode, set up conversion tracking before you spend anything, target a small group of high-intent keywords on exact and phrase match, add negative keywords on day one, and send clicks to a landing page that matches the search. Then set your budget from the maximum you can pay per lead, not from a guess, and check the search terms report every week.

This guide walks through those steps in order, with a worked budget example, a pre-launch checklist and the mistakes that waste most beginner budgets. It is written for small business owners, freelancers and new media buyers running their first account.

How does Google Ads work?

You choose keywords that describe what people search for when they need you, write ads, and set a budget. When someone searches, Google runs an auction among advertisers whose keywords match. Who shows, and in what position, depends on your bid and on the quality and relevance of your ad and landing page. You pay when someone clicks (on Search campaigns using standard cost-per-click bidding), not when the ad is shown.

For beginners, Search campaigns are the best place to start because they capture people who are already looking. Display, YouTube, Demand Gen and Performance Max can come later, once you know your numbers and have conversion data.

Step 1: Set up the account properly

  • Switch to Expert Mode. The simplified setup (Smart campaigns) hides the controls you need: keyword match types, search terms and negative keywords. Look for "Switch to Expert Mode" during sign-up.
  • Set the time zone and currency carefully. They cannot be changed later without creating a new account.
  • Turn on two-step verification and add a second admin so you are never locked out.
  • Link Google Analytics 4 so you can see what visitors do after the click.
  • Uncheck the extras. When creating your first Search campaign, untick the Display Network. In the networks setting, consider leaving Search Partners off until you have data to compare.
  • Check AI Max settings. Since 1 September 2026, Google has been upgrading Search campaigns that used campaign-level broad match or automatically created assets to AI Max for Search, with search term matching switched on. For a first campaign with no conversion data, review these settings in the campaign and keep your keyword targeting tight until you know which searches convert.

Step 2: Track conversions before you spend

Without conversion tracking, Google's bidding has nothing to learn from and you cannot tell good keywords from bad ones. Set up, at minimum:

  • Form submissions — a conversion that fires on the thank-you page or on a successful form submit event, using the Google tag or Google Tag Manager.
  • Calls from ads — use a call asset and set a minimum call length that represents a real enquiry; many service businesses use 60–120 seconds.
  • Calls from your website — a website call conversion using a Google forwarding number, or your call tracking tool's integration.

Mark only real business outcomes (leads, calls, purchases) as primary conversions. Page views, scrolls and "time on site" should be secondary or not tracked at all, because Smart Bidding optimizes toward whatever you mark as primary. If you sell online, also turn on enhanced conversions. Our Analytics, Tracking & Automation course shows each setup click by click.

Step 3: Choose keywords with buying intent

Not every relevant search is a buying search. "Emergency plumber near me" and "boiler repair cost" signal someone ready to pay; "how does a boiler work" usually does not. Sort keyword ideas into three groups and start with the first:

IntentExample searchesUse in first campaign?
High — ready to buy or call"[service] near me", "[service] [city]", "hire [service]", "[service] quote", "emergency [service]"Yes
Medium — comparing"best [service] [city]", "[service] cost", "[service] reviews"Test once the first group works
Low — researching"how to", "what is", "DIY", "free"No — add many as negatives

Group keywords into tight themes of roughly 5–20 closely related terms per ad group (for example, one ad group for "boiler repair", another for "boiler installation"). That lets each ad and landing page match the search closely.

Which match type should beginners use?

Google's match types control how closely a search must match your keyword:

  • Exact match [boiler repair] — searches with the same meaning or intent.
  • Phrase match "boiler repair" — searches that include the meaning of your keyword.
  • Broad match — searches related to your keyword, which can reach much further.

Start with exact and phrase match. Broad match works best when Smart Bidding has plenty of conversion data to learn from, which a brand-new account does not have. For a deeper keyword process, see our guide to keyword research for pay per call; the method works for any lead-generation campaign.

Step 4: Add negative keywords on day one

Negative keywords stop your ads showing for searches you do not want to pay for. Build a starter list before launch and apply it at campaign or account level. Common starters for service businesses:

  • Job seekers: jobs, salary, hiring, career, apprenticeship, training
  • Free and DIY: free, diy, how to, tutorial, youtube, video
  • Learning: course, certification, school, pdf
  • Wrong product or service: anything you do not offer, competitors' brand names if you do not want them

Then grow the list every week from the search terms report (Step 8).

Step 5: Write ads that match the search

Responsive search ads let you write up to 15 headlines (30 characters each) and 4 descriptions (90 characters each), which Google mixes and matches. Write headlines in these slots so any combination still makes sense:

SlotPurposeExample
1–3Keyword themeBoiler Repair in Leeds
4–6Main benefitSame-Day Engineer Visits
7–9ProofGas Safe Registered Team
10–12Offer or price pointFixed Call-Out Price
13–15Qualifier or call to actionCall Now for a Free Quote

Add assets too: sitelinks to your key pages, callouts (guarantees, opening hours), structured snippets (service types) and a call asset if phone calls matter. Only use claims you can back up. The Google Ad lab lets you draft and preview headlines against character limits, and our Copywriting That Sells course covers the formulas.

Step 6: Send clicks to a matching landing page

Sending every click to your homepage is one of the most expensive beginner mistakes. Use one landing page per keyword theme, with:

  • A headline that repeats the searcher's need ("Boiler repair in Leeds, usually same day").
  • One clear call to action — a short form, a click-to-call button on mobile, or both.
  • Proof near the top: reviews, accreditations, photos of real work.
  • Fast loading on mobile, and no menus that lead visitors away.

Step 7: Set budget and bidding from your numbers

Work backwards from what a customer is worth:

  1. Profit per customer: average first-sale profit (or lifetime profit, if repeat business is reliable).
  2. Close rate: what share of leads become customers.
  3. Maximum cost per lead = profit per customer × close rate. This is break-even; aim well below it.
  4. Maximum cost per click = maximum cost per lead × landing page conversion rate.

The Max CPL lab and PPC Profit lab do this math for you.

Which bid strategy should a beginner use?

Two sensible starting points:

  • Maximize Conversions, ideally with a maximum CPC limit if your campaign offers one, once conversion tracking is working. It lets Google bid for the searches most likely to convert.
  • Manual CPC with tight bids, if you want full control while you learn, or if tracking is limited.

After roughly 30 or more conversions, consider setting a target CPA close to the cost per lead you are actually achieving, then lower it gradually. Setting a target far below reality usually just stops the campaign from spending.

Budget-wise, the daily budget should comfortably buy at least 10 clicks a day at your expected cost per click, otherwise data comes in too slowly to make decisions.

Step 8: Check the account every week

A 20-minute weekly routine catches most problems:

  1. Search terms report: add irrelevant searches as negatives; add strong converting searches as new exact-match keywords.
  2. Keywords: pause keywords that have spent more than your maximum cost per lead with no conversions (give each a fair amount of spend first).
  3. Ads: look at asset performance and replace the weakest headlines.
  4. Budget: move budget toward ad groups or campaigns delivering leads below your target cost.
  5. Lead quality: ask sales or check the CRM — cheap leads that never buy are not cheap.

Worked example: a $1,000 first month

An illustrative home-repair business (the figures are assumptions, not benchmarks — use your own):

InputValueResult
Average profit per job$400
Close rate (leads to jobs)25%Break-even cost per lead = $400 × 0.25 = $100
Target cost per lead (half of break-even)$50
Landing page conversion rate8%Break-even CPC = $100 × 0.08 = $8; target CPC = $50 × 0.08 = $4
Monthly budget$1,000 (about $33/day)At $4 per click ≈ 250 clicks ≈ 20 leads ≈ 5 jobs ≈ $2,000 profit before ad cost

If actual clicks cost $9, the campaign cannot break even at an 8% conversion rate. The fix is not a bigger budget: improve the landing page conversion rate, narrow to higher-intent keywords, or raise the value per job. For how this connects to return on ad spend, read What Is ROAS?.

Pre-launch checklist

  • Expert Mode account, correct time zone and currency, two admins
  • GA4 linked; primary conversions = leads, calls or sales only
  • Test conversion recorded (submit your own form, make a test call)
  • Search campaign only; Display Network off
  • Location targeting set to "presence" (people in or regularly in your area) if you only serve locally
  • Ad schedule matches when you can answer calls
  • Ad groups of 5–20 tightly related exact/phrase keywords
  • Negative keyword list applied
  • One responsive search ad per ad group with all 15 headlines, plus sitelinks, callouts and a call asset
  • Matching landing page per theme, tested on a phone
  • Daily budget buys at least 10 clicks at your expected CPC
  • Maximum cost per lead written down before launch

Common beginner mistakes

  • Launching without conversion tracking — you cannot optimize what you cannot see.
  • Using broad match with no data — budget drifts into loosely related searches.
  • Accepting every recommendation — some raise spend more than results. Read each one and decide.
  • Changing everything every day — give changes a week of data before judging.
  • Ignoring lead quality — track which leads become customers, not just form fills.
  • Running ads when nobody answers the phone — schedule ads for staffed hours.

Frequently asked questions

How much should a beginner spend on Google Ads?

Enough to buy at least 10 clicks a day at the typical cost per click in your niche, for at least a month. Google Keyword Planner shows estimated bid ranges; multiply the top-of-page estimate by 10 and by 30 for a rough monthly minimum. If that is not affordable, narrow your keywords or locations rather than spreading a small budget thinly.

How long until Google Ads works?

Ads can show within hours of approval, but expect two to four weeks before you have enough data to judge keywords and ads with confidence, and longer for Smart Bidding to settle.

Should I use Performance Max as a beginner?

Usually not first. Performance Max spreads budget across Search, YouTube, Display, Gmail, Discover and Maps with less visibility into what is working. Start with Search, learn your numbers, then test Performance Max once you have steady conversions.

Is Smart Bidding better than manual bidding?

Smart Bidding usually performs well once it has reliable conversion data. With little or no data, manual CPC or Maximize Conversions with bid limits gives you more control while you learn.

Can I run Google Ads without a website?

Call-focused campaigns using call assets can work without a full site, but a fast, relevant landing page almost always improves results and gives Google more to judge relevance on.

Can Google Ads lead to a career?

Yes. Running campaigns profitably is the core skill of a paid search specialist or media buyer. Our guide on how to become a media buyer explains how to turn one well-documented campaign into a portfolio.
